Read this before giving your file to anyone

An XCTrack backup carries your name, your glider, your paired sensors, your waypoint files — down to the name of the competition you are flying. Never send it as it is: not to another pilot, not to a forum, not with a bug report. When you save, two of the three outcomes produce a shareable file: All your settings, minus what identifies you keeps your settings and replaces what names you, Shareable version, no personal data carries nothing but your pages. In both cases, read through the list of replacements the tool shows you before you download. Chapter 10 explains all of this in detail.

1.What this tool does, and what it does not do

Here you open a file exported by your instrument. Your pages appear exactly as the device draws them, at their real size. You change them, and you get back a fresh file to put on the instrument.

Three things worth knowing straight away

This tool does not talk to your instrument. There is no cable, no Bluetooth, no synchronisation. It produces a file that you transfer yourself, by SD card or by cable. Until you have made that transfer, your device knows nothing of what you changed here.

Nothing is simulated. The values you see in the widgets — an altitude, a speed, a sink rate — are fixed examples, written once and for all. They are there to judge the size and the placement of a widget, never to predict what it will show in flight.

Your file does not leave your machine. Everything happens in your browser: there is no server, no account, nothing sent. The page is made of static files, it has no one to talk to.

What does not move in your file

A file opened and then saved again without having been changed comes back out identical, byte for byte. And when you do change something, only what you changed changes: moving a widget rewrites its four coordinates and nothing else, the rest of the file comes out untouched.

This is what matters most, and here is why. XCTrack’s format is undocumented and gains settings with every release. An editor that rebuilt the file from what it managed to read would lose the rest without a word. This one carries through, exactly as it is, everything it does not understand: your particular settings survive, including the ones this editor shows nowhere.

What it will never do for you

  • It does not rearrange your pages and does not suggest a better layout.
  • It flags what looks doubtful to it, but corrects nothing on its own.
  • The only time it offers to take something out of your file is the cleanup in chapter 9 — and you have to see the list and click.

2.Getting the file off the instrument and opening it here

On the instrument

Go into XCTrack’s settings, then Export configuration. The file lands on the SD card. Get it onto your computer or your phone.

Two formats, two contents

XCTrack can write two sorts of export, and the difference matters:

  • a “backup” export carries your whole configuration: the pages and the device settings (vario, units, sensors, airspaces, your name, your glider…);
  • a “pages” export carries nothing but the widget pages. Imported back, it replaces the screens and touches nothing else.

The file extension says something else: .xcfg is the file on its own, .xczfg is an archive that carries extra files with it. Both open here.

A “pages” export is not anonymous

You often read that a “pages” export contains nothing personal. That is wrong. The title you wrote on a widget, the contents of a free-text widget, the name and the phone number stored in a call button all travel with the pages. The “pages” format is a coarse sort, it is not a cleanup.

Opening the file here

  1. Drop the file anywhere on the page — a frame appears saying Drop the file to open it;
  2. or click the drop area on the welcome screen;
  3. or, if a file is already open, take the File menu at the top right, then Open a file….

A file always opens in view-only mode: you look at it before you change it.

Opening a file when the previous one has been changed

Only one file is open at a time: opening another closes this one. As long as you have changed nothing, that happens without a word — there is nothing to lose.

As soon as you have changed something, the tool stops and asks you, under the title Your changes are not saved. The box reminds you of the names of both files and names your most recent change, using the very label of the undo step — for example “Set Volume — Vario”. Two outcomes:

  • Keep my changes — this is the default choice, the one the Enter key takes and the one the Escape key takes. Nothing has been touched: you get back your page, the widget you had selected and your undo history, all intact.
  • Open “…” and lose them — the other file takes the place, and what you had changed is recoverable nowhere.

To lose nothing: keep your changes, then Save the changes at the top of the page, or store the configuration in the library (chapter 11). Loading an entry from the library asks the same question, with the extra outcome Store first, then load.

If the file refuses to open

Two distinct screens can appear. This file could not be opened means the tool could make nothing of it: check that it really is an XCTrack export. This file could not be read means it was recognised but is damaged or incomplete. In both cases your file has not been changed, and Save a copy gives it back to you exactly as it went in. The Technical detail panel contains the text to copy into a bug report.

An unreadable file never replaces work in progress. If you drop a damaged file while the open file carries unsaved changes, the tool does not open it at all: it tells you so in a box — This file could not be read, with its Technical detail — and your file stays open exactly as it was. No confirmation is asked of you in that case, since there is nothing to decide.

3.Looking at your pages without changing anything

As long as you have not pressed Edit the pages, nothing you do writes anything at all. You can open everything, read everything, browse everything.

The overview

It shows all your pages, in two sections: Landscape then Portrait. A file always describes both; your instrument switches from one to the other.

Pages are numbered from 1. A page has no name: its position is its only identity, and it is what decides what appears when you press “next page” in flight.

What decides when your instrument shows a page is not its type, but the navigations it is enabled for — the five icons in XCTrack’s “Enable / Disable” box. The Manage the pages window says so page by page: Shown for every navigation, Shown for no navigation, or the list of the ones that bring it up. A page enabled for no navigation is skipped when you scroll — observed on an AIR³ — and the pre-flight check flags it for you.

An earlier version of this tool marked certain pages “hidden outside flight” according to their type, and counted how many your device would show on the ground. That was wrong: a trial on the instrument showed that the thermal assistant page does come back into the scroll on the ground. The mark and the count have been removed.

The information bar

Above the pages, a few facts read from the file: Format (file or archive), Device named by the file (or not declared), XCTrack labels (the language the widget names are shown in, and where it comes from). If the file carries no preferences, a Drawing settings line says so: the theme and the units used to draw are then factory values, not yours.

What the tool flags when it opens a file

Two blocks can appear, and they do not carry the same weight.

Worth checking in this file stays unfolded: it describes a fault. For instance a widget of zero width or height, a widget entirely hidden under another one that will therefore never show anything, or a part of the file the tool did not recognise.

The folded line below it gathers what merely informs without asking for anything: the export type, the language of the labels, the external files your configuration points to (map theme, waypoints, airspaces — they are on the original device, not in the file), the gap between the release that wrote the file and the one this editor is set to, or a display theme the editor cannot draw. Click the line to unfold it.

Overlaps are not flagged

Two widgets covering each other is common and often deliberate — it is even a competition technique. Flagging them would be noise, and a noisy warning teaches you to ignore the others. Only a widget covered entirely is flagged.

The pre-flight check

Alongside the findings that speak about the file, the same two blocks carry seven checks that speak about your pages in flight:

  • a widget entirely covered by the ones drawn after it, which a press therefore cannot reach — even though it is still visible;
  • a page that will never be shown, because no navigation type is ticked for it;
  • several thermal assistant pages in the same orientation: the instrument aims at only one of them when it switches to spiralling on its own, and which one remains a guess of this tool;
  • a widget perhaps too small to be read at arm’s length;
  • a Pro widget in a file that declares no licence;
  • two road maps on the same page, which XCTrack announces as impossible;
  • a setting written by an earlier release of XCTrack.

They are worked out on what you have in front of you: changing the Screen template recomputes the millimetres, and moving a widget recomputes the covering.

“To be confirmed on the instrument” means what it says

Four of these seven checks rest on a guess that nobody has yet verified on an AIR³: their title then carries the words to be confirmed on the instrument, and their explanation says what would settle the question. They never rise into the unfolded block — a guess has no business being displayed in the same tone as a measurement.

What the tool can already say some other way is not said twice: an action button deliberately slipped under a map is announced once, as an arrangement that works, and not a second time as a fault.

Opening a page and reading a widget

Click a thumbnail. The page appears on its own, at its real size. Previous page and Next page take you from one to the next — the left and right arrow keys do the same. ← All pages and the Escape key take you back.

Hover over a widget: its name and its dimensions in millimetres appear under the page. Click it: the bottom bar shows Widgets on the page and all its settings, read-only. Nothing there can be changed: this is view-only mode. Escape deselects.

The Widgets on the page list is ordered from the back to the front. It doubles as a way in: a widget marked unreachable here is entirely covered by another one, no click on the page reaches it, and this list is the only path to it. On the instrument it stays where it is — a button covered this way still answers to your finger.

4.The screen template and the zoom

Choosing the template

The Screen template menu, under the top bar, decides the geometry and the size in millimetres. It offers the three known AIR³ models, five common screen ratios, a Responsive mode where you give a width and a height in pixels yourself, and Add a device… to record yours once and for all.

On opening, the tool keeps the template the file declares. If it recognises nothing, it takes the AIR³ 7.2 and says so in the information bar.

This choice is a display setting: it is never written into your file. Changing it changes nothing. All that counts is the screen ratio, which decides the geometry, and the diagonal in inches, which decides the size in millimetres — the pixels have no effect at all, since widget positions are expressed as fractions of the screen.

Setting the zoom once and for all

The page is drawn at its real physical size. But your screen does not necessarily have the density the browser assumes. The Zoom slider corrects that, and Zoom 100% puts it back to its starting value.

  1. Hold a real ruler against your screen, next to the graduated ruler shown above the page.
  2. Drag the zoom slider until the two coincide.
  3. That is it: the millimetres announced for each widget are now the real ones.

It is that one setting that makes the question “will this text be readable at arm’s length, with gloves on?” useful. It is answered in millimetres, not in pixels.

5.Changing your pages

Press Edit the pages, at the top right. The word editing appears next to the name of the application, and an editing bar is added under the page. To come back out, press View only: nothing is undone.

Nothing goes to your disk until you have pressed Save the changes — see chapter 10.

Moving and resizing

Click a widget: it is selected, and the move starts straight away. That is more direct than on the device, which asks you to aim at a small target.

On the selected widget, nine handles appear: four corner brackets, four segments in the middle of the sides, and a dotted ellipse in the centre. The brackets and the segments resize; the ellipse moves. The central ellipse is also the only handle of a widget that others cover.

During the gesture you see two rectangles: the one your finger or your mouse is drawing, and the one where the widget will actually land. The gap between the two is the snap to the device’s grid — the number of cells is written in the editing bar, for example Grid 48 × 29. A widget pushed against an edge sticks to the edge. A gesture that goes too far is brought back into the page: it never fails.

While you resize, the size is shown in millimetres. The Widgets on the page list follows the gesture: as soon as it is finished, it carries the new size and the locator thumbnail shows the new rectangle. The unreachable here marks follow too — spreading a widget out can wall another one in, shrinking it can free one.

The bar of the selected widget

It sits above the widget, or below it if there is no room, and never hides it. It carries its stacking position — for example Layer 4 of 6 — four stacking buttons (send to back, send backward, bring forward, bring to front), then Duplicate and Delete.

The copy of a widget lands just in front of the original, offset by one cell. Deleting asks for no confirmation: Undo undoes it entirely.

Adding a widget

The Add a widget button opens the complete list, grouped by family, in the order of XCTrack’s own screen. Each line shows a preview, the official name of the widget and the name it carries in the file. The Search for a widget field filters on both.

One click drops the widget in the middle of the page. The border of the preview says what is going to happen:

  • solid border — this type already exists in your file: the widget will be copied from that example, with all its settings, including the ones this editor cannot present;
  • dotted border — this type is new to your file: the widget will be created with its basic settings only, XCTrack adding the others when it reads the file.

The Already in the file tickbox keeps only the types of the first case. Some lines carry a Pro badge: XCTrack reserves those widgets for the Pro licence.

Two preview notes do not mean the same thing. nothing at rest is a fact about the device: this widget paints nothing until data has arrived, and yet it takes up its space. preview not drawn is a limit of this editor: it has no dedicated drawing for that type, and prefers to show an honest frame rather than a misleading approximation.

Managing the pages

The Manage the pages button opens the two carousels, landscape and portrait. You can:

  • insert a page: the + button between two thumbnails unfolds a panel where you choose the type — thermal assistant, XC assistant, competition, or blank;
  • duplicate a page: the copy lands just after it;
  • delete a page: the first click shows the consequences and changes the button into Confirm deletion, the second one deletes;
  • reorder: drag one thumbnail onto another, or use the two arrows.

Before each gesture, the tool says what is going to shift: “Pages 3 to 5 become 4 to 6”. It also warns you if you are about to create a second thermal assistant page — this tool assumes that XCTrack only switches automatically to the last one, without having checked it — or to leave nothing but pages enabled for no navigation, in which case your instrument would have no page left to show in that orientation.

The type of a page cannot be changed here. XCTrack fixes it when the page is created, and the effect of changing it afterwards has not been checked on the device: we would rather not offer it than offer it without knowing.

Undo and redo

The two boxed arrows, at the top right, undo and redo. Hover over them: they say what they are going to do — “Undo: Move GPS altitude”. From the keyboard, Ctrl+Z undoes and Ctrl+Shift+Z redoes (Cmd on a Mac).

Everything is covered: gestures on widgets, additions, deletions, stacking, and all page operations. The last hundred steps are kept; beyond that, the oldest are forgotten. A gesture with no effect produces no step.

One exception: the library is not covered. Deleting a stored configuration cannot be undone.

The keyboard gestures

  • Arrows — move by one cell
  • Shift + arrows — resize by one cell
  • Ctrl + up / down arrow — bring forward or send backward one layer
  • Ctrl + Shift + up / down arrow — bring to front, send to back
  • Ctrl + D — duplicate
  • Delete — remove
  • Escape — abandon the gesture under way, otherwise deselect

6.The settings of a widget

Choose a widget, on the page or in the list: its settings appear in the bottom bar, in the order the file carries them. The handle at the top of the bar changes its height; the fold button closes it.

The fold holds. The bar arrives folded and unfolds on its own at your first selection, because it then has something to show. But as soon as you touch the Fold away button, you are the one deciding: no selection will reopen it, and it leaves you the whole page for as long as you want. Folded, its header bar still names the widget you chose, and its button says what unfolding would give you — Unfold the settings, or Widget list if nothing is selected.

Beyond a dozen settings, a Filter the settings field appears. It searches in the label and in the name the setting carries in the file. The ? button next to a setting unfolds the explanation XCTrack gives of it.

In view-only mode: what has been set

Outside editing mode, a bar announces how many settings differ from what XCTrack puts on a fresh widget, and the Only what differs button keeps only those. Each line carries its mark: set by you, factory value, or factory value unknown when our survey says nothing about that setting.

The settings the widget does not write

At the end of the panel, a block gathers the settings that are not in your file for this widget. XCTrack applies to them the value from its own code — the one written next to them. That is not the same thing as a setting written at that value: see the next chapter.

7.The three gestures around a factory value

The factory value is what XCTrack applies when nothing is written in the file. Three gestures revolve around it. They are not equivalent, and this is the most important thing in this manual after personal data.

“Set this value” and “Write this value” — writing an absent factory value

The setting is not in the file. This button writes it in, at its factory value. It is offered on both screens, but under two different names: it is Set this value in the panel of a widget, and Write this value in the general settings.

On a widget, it changes nothing about what your device does today: it was already applying that value. What it changes is for later — the value is now pinned, and an XCTrack update that changed that factory value will no longer change your setting.

“Remove” — silencing the file on a setting

This button exists only in the general settings, and only when the value written in the file already equals the factory value. It then removes the line: the file says nothing more about that setting.

It never appears on a value you chose. Silencing the file on a deliberate setting would deprive you of that setting, and a discreet button must not do that in one click.

“Remove” is not a return to the factory value

Removing a general setting from the file does not put your device back to the factory value. It will keep the one it already has. See the box in chapter 8.

“Restore the factory value” — the only one that erases a choice

This gesture replaces a value you chose with the one a fresh XCTrack applies. It is the only one of the three that erases a deliberate setting: the other two only touch values that already equalled the factory one, or that were not written at all.

So it does not hide. It takes its own line under the setting, and shows both values side by side before the click: “this factory value, this value in this file. Restoring changes what the device does in flight.” If the factory value was surveyed on an XCTrack release other than that of your file, the sentence says so too.

It writes the factory value rather than erasing the line. The setting then moves to the “factory value” state, from where Remove becomes available. If you want to go all the way, that is a second deliberate click, and you see the two effects separately.

When no button is offered

It happens that none of the three is offered, and that is deliberate. Writing a guessed value would be worse than offering nothing. The cases:

  • the factory value has not been surveyed;
  • XCTrack works it out at start-up from the language and the country of the device — that is the case for the units;
  • XCTrack publishes two that contradict each other: its code and its settings screen do not say the same thing. The tool then shows both and does not choose;
  • the surveyed factory value is a compound value, and this editor only writes simple values.

8.XCTrack’s general settings

The Settings button in the top bar opens everything that is set outside the widget pages: units, buttons, sensors, sound, airspaces, Livetracking. These are the 217 settings XCTrack knows, presented in the order of the twenty screens of its menu.

To change them, press Edit the settings. In view-only mode, no input field is built at all: this is not a greying-out, there is simply nothing to touch.

These settings exist only in a “backup” export. If you have opened a “pages” export, the page will tell you: this is not a loss, your file simply does not contain them.

Finding your way

A bar at the top counts what you have set, what is still at the factory value, and what is missing from the file. Three commands help you sort:

  • Filter the settings searches in the label, in the name of the setting and in its value; accents and capitals make no difference;
  • Only what I have set keeps only what differs from the factory;
  • Hide personal values replaces with dots the values that identify you — useful if someone is looking at your screen.

The measured box, not to be missed

An absent setting is not put back to the factory value

On a Replace everything import, your device keeps the setting it already has: what the file does not say is not touched. The import is a merge, not a replacement.

This is a measurement, not a guess. The protocol: a display setting was removed from a backup, the backup was re-imported with “Replace everything”, then the configuration was exported again. The setting had come back to the value the device already carried, and not to the factory value. A second setting, changed in the same round, proved that the import had indeed written the rest.

Two limits, which must be said: the measurement covers the “Replace everything” mode, the two other import modes have not been tested; and the case of a brand-new device has not been tested — on a device that has never touched that setting, the factory value necessarily applies, but that is a deduction.

Careful: on the widget side it is the other way round. XCTrack fills in, when it reads the file, the settings a widget does not carry, and the factory value does apply there. The two situations were measured separately, and they do not give the same result.

What can be set, and what cannot

Most lines can be set: tickbox, list, slider, number, text, colour. Some stay on display with no control, and each one says why:

  • the ones that open a dialog on the device — the buttons to press, the acoustic vario table: “a key to press on the instrument, a table to build” cannot be entered here;
  • the ones whose value is compound: the page shows it as it is, without opening it, and never rewrites it;
  • the ones XCTrack names nowhere in readable form: with no label, the tool does not offer to change them;
  • the ones that record the state of the application rather than a setting.

Three blocks at the end of the page gather these cases: Settings with no label, What the application has remembered (not settings), and Lines this catalogue does not know — those last ones were written by another release of XCTrack. All of them come back out untouched.

The personal data panel

A folded panel, at the top of the page, inventories what these settings reveal about you, with the nature of each one and where the tool gets it from. It counts the general settings only; the texts you wrote inside the widgets are inventoried elsewhere, when you save.

9.The release you are targeting, compatibility and the cleanup

File menu, then Version and compatibility….

XCTrack’s format changes with every release. This screen asks you for The XCTrack release you are targeting — the one on the device you will import this file back into — and then tells you what your file carries that this release no longer reads, and what it expects that the file does not have.

This is a statement of fact: nothing moves until you decide it should.

Choosing your release

You choose by the name your device shows, “1.0.3-beta”. The release your file declares is already preselected, and a sentence says where that preselection comes from — and if it is arbitrary, it says that too.

Since several releases often accept exactly the same settings, the tool names the ones it cannot tell apart from yours: choosing between two neighbours then has no effect, and it is better to say so than to leave you guessing.

The three words of the diagnosis

Every remark carries one of these words, and it governs what to do:

  • outdated — XCTrack no longer reads this setting, and real files written by that very release carry it anyway. We have seen it happen, we are not assuming it. Removing it is defensible.
  • blind spot — the gap is ours, not your file’s: our reading of the releases has a hole at that point. We do not touch it.
  • unknown — no release we have been able to read carries this setting, and no real file shows it. We conclude nothing.

The diagnosis sorts the gaps into eight families, and each one spells out what to do. The one that matters most is perhaps this one: Our reading has a hole: the setting did exist — “Never remove them. These are valid settings, and taking them for outdated ones would erase yours.”

Why the tool refuses to touch certain settings

It all rests on a simple imbalance: removing nothing breaks nothing, removing wrongly breaks a flight configuration.

A database that took every observed setting for a valid setting would protect exactly the leftovers a cleanup is meant to take out. A database that took every unread setting for a withdrawn setting would delete perfectly valid settings. So the tool distinguishes three things: what it has read in the releases, what a real file carries, and why the two differ.

The sorting is deliberately narrow. Of the eight families of gap, only one is offered for deletion: the one a real file attests to. And even there, an outdated setting the tool cannot say since which release has stopped being used stays in place — we do not offer to delete what we would not be able to explain.

Removing what an older release left behind

XCTrack keeps the settings it no longer knows: a backup from this year still drags along switches from three years ago. The Remove what an older release left behind section, under the diagnosis and only in editing mode, offers to take them out. If there is nothing to remove, the section does not appear at all.

It starts by reassuring you, and it is true: “Nothing is urgent and nothing is broken: XCTrack carries them along without reading them, and leaving them there changes nothing on your pages. Removing them makes the file smaller, that is all.”

  1. Unfold See these 9 settings, and untick what you would rather keep — the number is the one from your file. Everything is ticked to start with; each line says up to which XCTrack release the setting was still read, and its value when that value is simple.
  2. Untick what you want to keep. The count updates, and the button says exactly how many settings it is going to remove.
  3. Click the button. The panel is replaced by a message and by Put these 9 settings back.

Going back is complete: put back, the file comes out byte for byte. The gesture is also recorded in the history, so Undo undoes it like everything else.

And nothing has gone anywhere until you have saved: “Your device knows nothing of it yet: the file only changes when you save it.”

10.Saving, and choosing what you hand over

The save button, at the top right, says what is going to happen: Save a copy when you have touched nothing, Save the changes when the document has changed. It is also the only visible sign that work is under way.

It opens the Save this configuration box, which asks you one single thing: what should be saved?

Three answers, ordered by how much travels: everything, everything except what identifies you, your pages alone. Going down a step always means “giving less”. Each outcome carries, under its card, the inventory of what it will do — it appears when you tick it, and before any download.

The file name is settled in advance

It is timestamped and keeps nothing of the original name, which often contains a first name. It looks like xctrack_2026-08-21-153207_backup.xcfg. Since the date is written back to front, the alphabetical order of your files is their chronological order.

The name also carries the format of the file produced and the word -anon when values have been replaced: …_backup-anon.xcfg is a whole backup, …_pages-anon.xcfg carries pages only. Do not mix them up: a backup imports settings back, a pages does not.

Outcome 1 — “Your configuration, exactly as it is”

This is your file. If you have changed nothing, it comes back out exactly as it went in, without a single comma rewritten — and its SHA-256 hash is that of the original file, you can check for yourself. If you have changed something, everything you did not touch is copied out identically, down to the numbers and the original spacing.

This is the outcome to choose to put the file back on your instrument. The box then reminds you what it carries of you.

Outcome 2 — “All your settings, minus what identifies you”

The file stays a whole backup. Your vario and its sounds, your units, your theme, your airspace thresholds, your buttons: it is all there. What identifies you is replaced with a neutral value, or removed line by line.

This is the outcome to choose to ask for help with a setting — on a forum, in a message to another pilot. The other shareable version carries no setting at all: it cannot carry your question.

Every line dealt with is shown with its verdict and its reason, grouped by what happens to it:

  • Replaced with a neutral value — your name becomes “Pilot”, your glider “Glider”, the map theme you installed goes back to XCTrack’s factory value. The line keeps its place and its type;
  • Removed from the file — the whole line goes, because its value is a structure: your current task and its coordinates, your paired sensors and their Bluetooth addresses, your waypoint files, your offline maps, your Livetracking quick messages. Writing a replacement structure would amount to making up a shape XCTrack never writes; an absent line, on the other hand, is the state of a fresh installation;
  • Kept exactly as they are, and here is why — what the tool refused to touch, said as plainly as the rest: your Livetrack broadcasting choices, the category of your glider. These are settings, not data: they carry no name, no number, no address, and they are often exactly what you have come to talk about;
  • Present in the file, but empty — the slot exists and carries nothing. There is nothing to replace in it.

On this project’s reference backup, that gives 16 personal settings: 3 replaced, 4 removed, 4 kept, 5 empty.

What the recipient will not get is the removed lines — and none of them is a setting: they are files and devices that live at your place, which they could have done nothing with.

This outcome does not appear on a file that carries no preferences — a “pages” export, for instance. It would produce there exactly the same file as outcome 3, under the same name, and two indistinguishable cards are worth less than one card fewer.

This outcome rests on a list, and a list goes out of date

It deals with the 44 known personal settings of XCTrack and the eleven text fields of the widgets. The format changes with every release: a personal setting that has appeared since would not be on the list, and would go out in the clear. Outcome 3, further down, depends on no list at all — it carries no setting whatsoever.

Outcome 3 — “Shareable version, no personal data”

This is a “pages” export whose texts written by you are replaced with neutral ones. The layout is kept; the preferences do not travel.

This is the safest outcome, and the only one that depends on no list of settings: the preferences are not taken out of it, they are never put into it. Choose it when you are sharing a layout — and outcome 2 when your question is about a setting.

Before the download, the tool shows you:

  • What will not travel — the whole sections that stay with you and, spelled out, what the recipient will have to set for themselves: their units, their theme, their vario, their airspaces, their Livetracking, their sensors;
  • The archive attachments, if your file is an .xczfg — they do not travel, and the shareable version is always written as a plain .xcfg;
  • Your texts inside the widgets — the inventory, line by line: where the text sits, what value it carried, what it is replaced with, and why.

Replacements keep the shape of what they replace, so as not to break your layout: a title becomes “Title 1”, a phone number becomes a number of the same pattern but impossible to dial, a web address becomes an address in a reserved domain that leads nowhere.

What looks like a text you would have written

Under each of the two shareable outcomes, the tool adds one last paragraph. In it, it flags the texts that are on none of its lists and yet look like something you would have written: a sentence, an accented word, a web or email address, a file path, a device address, a number.

It flags them, it does not replace them. Replacing on suspicion would damage legitimate settings — and only you know whether you wrote that text. On the real files of our trials, this paragraph flags nothing: what XCTrack writes itself is made of words picked from lists, never of sentences.

The inventory is the check, not our promise

The list of text fields dealt with is fixed, and XCTrack’s format changes with every release: a text field that has appeared since would go out in the clear. Read through the inventory before you send the file. An empty inventory does not prove an absence — it only proves that the tool found nothing among what it knows how to look for.

Putting the file back on the instrument

  1. Copy the downloaded file onto the instrument’s SD card, or transfer it by cable.
  2. On the instrument, open XCTrack’s configuration sharing screen and start the import.
  3. The Replace everything mode is the one our measurements cover. Remember what it does exactly: what the file does not say is not touched (chapter 8).
  4. Keep a backup of your previous configuration before importing. This tool can do nothing for you once the import is done.

11.The configuration library

File menu, then Library…. There you store several configurations under a name — one for competition, one for hike-and-fly, one for the school — and you come back to one of them whenever you want.

Everything stays in this browser. Nothing is sent anywhere, there is no account and no server, and another device does not see your library. The bytes stored are those of your file, never a rewritten copy: they are checked by digest every time they are read back.

Storing, finding, taking back out

  • Store the open configuration asks for a name — “the one you will recognise in six months” — and an optional note.
  • Store a file… stores a .xcfg taken from the disk, without opening it.
  • Load brings a configuration back into the editor. If the open document carries unsaved changes, the tool stops and offers to store it first.
  • Take the file back out gives you back the stored bytes, exactly as they went in.
  • Check the digest recomputes the digest and compares it with the one recorded when the entry was stored.
  • Identity card shows everything the entry carries, carefully separating what the file declares from what this editor assumes.

Each entry also shows its personal data count, in the form “16 pieces of personal data · 2 travel with the pages”. Both numbers are always given, zero included.

This is not a backup

Export your library

Clearing the site data erases the library, and some browsers erase by themselves the data of a site not visited for a few days. Export the library turns it into a ZIP archive from which each configuration can be extracted with any unzipping tool. Import a library… reads it back, without ever overwriting an existing entry.

The Stop the browser from erasing my library button asks the browser for durable storage. That is never a guarantee. The only backup that holds is the archive you export.

Finally: this library has no wastebasket. A deletion is final, and “Undo” can do nothing about it. That is why deleting asks for a confirmation in a box of its own.

12.What is measured, and what is not

This section is not a legal precaution. It is the most useful piece of information this manual can give you.

Where everything the tool knows comes from

The format of XCTrack files is not documented, and it changes with every release of the application. Everything this tool knows about it comes from two sources: real files observed over four years and eight releases, and the reading of the application itself. Nothing comes from a specification, because there is none.

That is what makes the tool valuable — and it is why it is built to carry through what it does not understand rather than to model the format.

Three things not to confuse

  • What has been observed on the device. One AIR³ 7.2, one release of XCTrack. The widget drawings, the snapping grid, the behaviour of an import. The tool says so when that is the case.
  • What has been read in the application. The names and descriptions of the widgets, the factory values, the list of settings. That is solid, but it is not the same thing as a measurement on a device that flies.
  • What is deduced. For example: the snapping grid was measured on a single device; on all the other templates it is extrapolated, and gives a plausible order of magnitude and nothing more.

What the rendering is worth

The widget drawings are reconstructed from what was observed on a single device and a single release. A widget whose drawing has not been reproduced is shown in an honest generic shape rather than in a misleading approximation. And if your file asks for a display theme the editor cannot draw, it tells you: the colours you see are not those of your device, but the geometry is right.

Two languages on screen, and that is not a mistake

The interface of this editor and this manual exist in five languages: French, English, Dutch, German, Spanish. The names of widgets and settings, on the other hand, do not follow that choice: they come from XCTrack, in the thirty-odd languages the application carries, and they follow the language of your file. So you can read the interface in Spanish and see your widgets named in French, if that is how your instrument names them. That is deliberate: those are the words you will find again in flight.

What the interface does not do yet

  • The library shows no image preview: the space is reserved, the thumbnail is an empty frame that says so.
  • The sorting of the general settings does not offer everything: what the catalogue has not surveyed is not offered for setting, rather than being guessed.

13.Giving feedback, reporting what is wrong

This tool is written for pilots, and it only improves through what they say about it. Suggestions and corrections go through GitHub issues:

https://github.com/frederict/xcfg-editor/issues

Everything helps: a badly drawn widget, a setting the editor does not show, an obscure word, an XCTrack release missing from the list, a file that refuses to open, a sentence in this manual that cost you time.

Saying which device, which XCTrack release and what you expected saves a great deal of time.

Write in your own language. The interface and this manual exist in French, English, Dutch, German and Spanish: an issue written in any of those five languages will be read. There is no need to switch to English to report something — and a translation that rings false, or that names a button differently from the screen, is exactly the kind of thing worth reporting.

Never attach your own file

A GitHub issue is public, and forever. Your .xcfg carries your name, your glider, your sensors, your waypoint files, sometimes your contact details.

If a file is indispensable to understand the problem, first produce a shareable version with this tool — Shareable version, no personal data if the problem is about a page, All your settings, minus what identifies you if it is about a setting. Read through the inventory of replacements it shows you, and send that one only.
